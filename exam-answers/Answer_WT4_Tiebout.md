## Past Exam Question

**GY457 Exam 2012, Q4 (Essay-style, 100 marks):**

> Consumer mobility and inter-jurisdictional competition lead to an efficient provision of local public services. Critically discuss the validity of the above proposition by reference to the relevant literature that followed Charles Tiebout's (1956, *Journal of Political Economy*) seminal paper.

---

## Merit-Level Answer

### Introduction

Tiebout (1956) proposed that the decentralised provision of local public goods can achieve allocative efficiency through residential mobility: households "vote with their feet" by relocating to the jurisdiction that best matches their preferred tax-service bundle. This generates inter-jurisdictional competition analogous to market competition among private firms. While theoretically elegant, the proposition depends on restrictive assumptions that are rarely met in practice. Post-Tiebout empirical work — particularly on fiscal capitalization — offers partial support for the sorting mechanism, but also reveals important limitations and regressive distributional consequences.

---

### 1. The Tiebout Mechanism

Tiebout's core argument is that efficient provision of local public goods emerges from three forces working together:

1. **Variety:** multiple jurisdictions offer differentiated tax-service packages.
2. **Sorting:** mobile households select the jurisdiction matching their preferences, revealed through location choices rather than voting.
3. **Competition:** jurisdictions compete for residents (and their tax base), disciplining inefficient provision.

The result mimics private market efficiency: each jurisdiction reaches its optimal scale and residents are served at the bundle they prefer, with no deadweight loss from preference misrepresentation.

**Key assumptions required for full efficiency:**

| Assumption | Content |
|---|---|
| Perfect information | Households know all jurisdictions' offerings |
| Costless mobility | No moving costs, no social network disruption |
| Many jurisdictions | Sufficient variety for preference matching |
| No spillovers | Public goods are purely local; no cross-boundary externalities |
| No scale economies | Optimal jurisdiction size is well-defined |

---

### 2. Fiscal Capitalization as an Empirical Test

The primary testable prediction of the Tiebout model is **fiscal capitalization**: differences in local public services and tax rates should be fully capitalised into property prices, since households bid up prices in high-service jurisdictions and bid down prices in high-tax ones.

**Oates (1969)** provided the first systematic test using New Jersey municipalities. He found:
- A £1,000 increase in property taxes → **£664 fall in house prices** (approximately 67% capitalization).
- Higher school spending → significantly higher property values.

This is broadly consistent with the Tiebout prediction that households value the net fiscal surplus (services minus taxes) and capitalize it into housing.

**Subsequent literature** confirms positive capitalization of school quality into house prices, with estimates ranging from **1.3% to 10% per standard deviation** improvement in school test scores (Gibbons et al. 2013; Cheshire & Sheppard 2004).

---

### 3. Supply Elasticity Moderates Capitalization

A critical insight from later work is that **housing supply elasticity conditions the degree of fiscal capitalization** (Hilber & Mayer 2009; Hilber et al. 2011).

**Hilber & Mayer (2009)** found:
- **Urban areas** (low undeveloped land, inelastic supply): school spending capitalization = **33%**
- **Rural areas** (abundant land, elastic supply): school spending capitalization = only **10%**

**Mechanism:** In supply-constrained markets, a demand shock (e.g., improved school quality) raises prices rather than quantities. Fiscal benefits accrue almost entirely to existing **homeowners and landlords**, not to renters or new entrants. This is the opposite of Tiebout's efficiency claim for renters.

```
Supply-inelastic market (e.g., London):
  Better school → demand ↑ → price ↑↑, quantity unchanged
  → Homeowners capture full fiscal surplus
  → Renters pay higher rent, net welfare gain for them = 0 or negative

Supply-elastic market (e.g., Houston):
  Better school → demand ↑ → quantity ↑, price modest ↑
  → Fiscal benefits spread to new residents via housing supply
```

This implies: **Tiebout sorting partially works, but benefits are not efficiently distributed.**

---

### 4. Critical Assessment of the Model

#### 4.1 Where Tiebout Works Partially

- **Sorting does occur:** household income and preferences are correlated with jurisdiction choice (Black 1999 shows sharp price discontinuities at school district boundaries, confirming valuation of school quality).
- **Competition incentivizes service quality:** jurisdictions with poor services lose residents, weakening their tax base, creating fiscal discipline.

#### 4.2 Where Tiebout Fails

**Assumption violations undermine efficiency:**

**(a) Mobility costs are high.** Moving involves financial costs (transaction costs, stamp duty), social capital losses, and job disruption. Lower-income households face higher relative mobility costs, meaning they cannot sort freely. The efficiency result breaks down when some households are effectively immobile.

**(b) Externalities exist.** Education generates positive spillovers across jurisdictions (a better-educated labor force benefits neighboring regions). Tiebout ignores this, leading to under-provision of services with positive spillovers.

**(c) Scale economies.** Natural monopoly arguments apply to some public goods (e.g., specialized hospitals, transit networks) where smaller jurisdictions cannot provide efficiently.

**(d) Strategic hold-out and race to the bottom.** In some contexts, inter-jurisdictional competition leads to a "race to the bottom" on business taxes, depleting public finance rather than improving it (Wilson 1986).

#### 4.3 Distributional Critique

Even if Tiebout sorting is efficient in aggregate, it has regressive distributional consequences:

- Sorting stratifies communities by income → richer jurisdictions provide better schools → **reinforces inequality across generations**.
- Government subsidies (e.g., central grants to improve schools in deprived areas) are largely capitalized into land values, benefiting **landowners** rather than the intended beneficiaries (Hilber et al. 2011).
- Policy implication: if the goal is to help low-income households, **place-based subsidies are inefficient in supply-constrained markets** — direct transfers to people are more effective ("help people, not places").

---

### 5. Conclusion

The Tiebout proposition contains an important kernel of truth: residential mobility and jurisdictional competition create incentives for efficient local public good provision, and fiscal capitalization evidence broadly confirms that households value the net fiscal surplus. However, full efficiency requires assumptions — costless mobility, no spillovers, no scale economies — that are routinely violated in practice. Supply constraints further distort the mechanism, concentrating fiscal benefits on existing homeowners rather than distributing them to residents broadly. The Tiebout model is thus best understood as a useful benchmark, not a description of reality: it illuminates the forces at work, while the departures from its assumptions explain much of the observed inequality in local public service provision.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Tiebout (1956)** | "Vote with feet" — households sort into jurisdictions matching tax-service preferences → efficiency via inter-jurisdictional competition |
| **Fiscal capitalization** | Local fiscal variables (taxes, school quality) embedded into property prices; test of Tiebout sorting |
| **Oates (1969)** | First systematic test: tax ↑£1,000 → house price −£664 (~67% capitalization) |
| **Hilber & Mayer (2009)** | Supply elasticity moderates capitalization: urban areas 33% vs rural 10% |
| **Homevoter (Fischel 2000)** | Homeowners lobby for restrictive zoning to protect asset values — Tiebout competition becomes captured |
| **Race to the bottom** | Jurisdictional competition on taxes may reduce public good provision below optimum |
| **"Help people, not places"** | Policy implication: place-based subsidies are captured by landowners in inelastic markets; direct transfers more efficient |

## 中文解题思路

**这道题的核心是批判性评估 Tiebout 模型的有效性。**

**答题结构：**
1. **解释机制**（Tiebout 怎么工作的）— 用脚投票 + 辖区竞争 + 5个关键假设
2. **支持证据**（财政资本化实证）— Oates (1969) 税收/学校质量的资本化
3. **供给弹性的调节作用**（关键创新点）— Hilber & Mayer 城市33%/农村10%；供给约束决定谁受益
4. **模型局限性**（批判性分析）— 流动成本高、外部性、规模经济、"向底部竞争"
5. **分配性批判**（高分要素）— 资本化使补贴变成房主红利，不帮助穷人
6. **结论：模型是有用基准，现实中假设被违反，效率仅部分成立**

**关键数字：**
- Oates: 税收 £1,000 → 房价 -£664 (~67%)
- Hilber & Mayer: 城市 33% vs 农村 10%
- 学校质量 1SD → 房价提升 1.3%-10%

**容易失分的地方：**
- 只描述 Tiebout 机制，不做批判分析
- 忘记供给弹性的调节作用（这是关键，区分了谁受益）
- 没有联系 "help people not places" 的政策含义
- 结论只重复不综合
