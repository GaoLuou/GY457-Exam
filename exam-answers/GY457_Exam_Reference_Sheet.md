# PART 1 — AT (Bagagli)

## AT ESSAY: Firms, CBD & Polycentric Cities
> Mock 2025/26 Q1 | Topics: AT1, AT4/5, AT6

### What the question asks
"Traditional urban economics models view cities as aligned around a single centre, the CBD. In this class of models, firms concentrate in the CBD despite paying higher rent. Critically assess this statement. Should we expect cities to become more polycentric?"

### Essay structure: Intro → 4 parts → Conclusion

**Introduction (2–3 sentences):**
The monocentric city model (Alonso 1964; Mills 1967; Muth 1969) provides the foundational framework for urban spatial structure, where land goes to the highest bidder and commercial users cluster near the centre. While this captures important mechanisms, it imposes a single exogenous agglomeration point and cannot explain secondary employment centres in modern cities. This essay critically assesses the CBD concentration argument and conditions for polycentricity.

---

**Part 1 — Why firms cluster in the CBD**

*Bid-rent logic:*
- Firm bid-rent = max rent willing to pay while earning zero profit
- Productivity spillovers decay with distance: **A_i = a · e^(−θD_i)**, where θ = spatial decay parameter
- High θ (knowledge-intensive firms) → steep bid-rent → strong CBD pull
- Since commercial bid-rent > residential bid-rent at the centre → land goes to commercial users

*Why specifically the CBD (not just any location):*
- **Knowledge spillovers** are extremely local — street/building level (Rosenthal & Strange 2020)
- **Labour market pooling** operates at the commuting-zone level
- Finance, law, professional services: high θ → cluster tightly in CBD
- Manufacturing: low θ → disperses

*Key empirical evidence:*
- Liotta et al. (2022): global rent gradient = **−1.4%/km** from CBD
- Heblich et al. (2020): removing London's historical rail network would reduce land values by **50%**

---

**Part 2 — Limitations: endogenous agglomeration**

The monocentric model treats CBD agglomeration as *exogenous*. Reality: firms generate bilateral spillovers with each other.

*Ahlfeldt & Wendland (2013)* — extended productivity function:
**A_i = a_i · e^(−θD_i) · e^(βZ_i)**, where Z_i = proximity-weighted output of all other firms

- Firms benefit from other firms → self-reinforcing clusters can form *anywhere*
- Creates **multiple equilibria**: initial conditions determine which cluster survives
- Analogous to Krugman (1991) core-periphery model: history matters

*Path dependence and historic anchoring:*
- NYC (3.4 million people in 1900, early subway) → dense monocentric CBD
- LA (only 100k in 1900, car-based) → dispersed polycentric structure
- Ahlfeldt et al. (2020): CBD dominance is historically locked in, not technologically determined

---

**Part 3 — Evidence for decentralisation and sub-centres**

*Employment decentralisation:*
- Glaeser & Kahn (2001): fewer than **29% of jobs** within 3 miles of CBD in most US metros
- Baum-Snow et al. (2017) — China: ring roads displaced **50% of industrial employment**
- Baum-Snow (2007): each additional highway through city centre reduces central city population by **18%**

*Sub-centres — organic formation (McMillen & Smith 2003):*
- Defined as locations where employment density exceeds the distance-predicted level
- Emerge when population ↑ and CBD congestion ↑ (Fujita & Ogawa 1982 model)
- Older, larger, more congested cities → more sub-centres

*Edge cities — developer-built (Henderson & Mitra 1996):*
- Single developer internalises agglomeration gains; chooses location optimising: CBD spillovers vs. lower land/wage costs
- Example: Tyson's Corner, VA — **28m sq ft** Class A office vs DC CBD's 17m
- Distinguishing feature: net commuting *destination* (more jobs than housing)

---

**Part 4 — Will cities become more polycentric?**

Yes, conditionally:
1. **Lower θ industries** (manufacturing, logistics) → already dispersed; trend continues
2. **High θ industries** (finance, tech, professional services) → CBD advantage persists
3. **Transport improvements (t↓)** → flatten bid-rent gradients → suburbanisation
4. **Rising CBD congestion** → decentralisation pressure
5. **Remote work** (post-pandemic) → may weaken CBD-specific agglomeration advantages

**Conclusion:** The monocentric model correctly identifies bid-rent logic and productivity basis for CBD concentration. However, it fails to account for endogenous/bilateral spillovers and multiple equilibria. Evidence confirms partial polycentricity globally, with sub-centres forming organically and edge cities built strategically. Whether polycentricity deepens depends on θ (spillover decay), congestion costs, and historical infrastructure lock-in.

---

## AT PARTITIONED: Suburbanisation, Reversal & Gentrification
> Mock 2025/26 Q2 | Topics: AT4/5, AT9

### Part (a) — Two AMM explanations for suburbanisation [30 marks]

**Key formula:** Bid-rent gradient = **∂p_q/∂x = −t/q**

**Mechanism 1 — Rising income (y↑):**
- Housing is a normal good: y↑ → q(y)↑ (households demand more space)
- Higher q → gradient −t/q becomes *less negative* (flatter)
- Bid-rent curve rotates: falls at centre, rises at suburbs → urban boundary x̄ expands
- Evidence: Baum-Snow (2007) — each additional highway → **−18% central population**

**Mechanism 2 — Falling commuting costs (t↓):**
- Directly flattens the gradient −t/q by the same mechanism
- Equivalent effect to income increase: suburbs become relatively cheaper
- Evidence: Heblich et al. (2020) — London steam railways triggered first mass suburbanisation

**Income segregation (important nuance):**
Under monetary commuting costs: richer households have higher q → flatter bid-rent → live in suburbs. Poor have steeper bid-rent → live centrally. Explains 20th century "white flight" pattern.

---

### Part (b) — One theory explaining BOTH suburbanisation AND reversal [40 marks]

**LeRoy & Sonstiele (1983) — 4-Stage Transportation Cost Cycle**

**Core assumption:** Income elasticity of marginal commuting cost > income elasticity of housing demand (rich value saved time more than bigger houses).

| Stage | Transport | Rich live | Poor live |
|-------|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| 1. Paradise | Slow, cheap only | Centre | Periphery |
| 2. Paradise Lost | Fast mode: expensive (rich only) | **Suburbs** | Centre |
| 3. Re-gentrification | Fast mode affordable for all | **Centre** (return) | Suburbs |
| 4. Paradise Regained | All use fast mode | Centre | Suburbs |

**US application:**
- 1900s: Cars expensive → only rich suburbanise → Stage 2 ("white flight")
- Late 20th century: Cars near-universal (92% of US households by 2017) → Stage 3
- Rich return to city centres: higher time value + urban amenities (non-tradeable goods: restaurants, culture — Couture & Handbury 2020)

**Why the reversal mechanism works:**
Once fast transport is universal, rich no longer gain differential commuting advantage. Their high time value makes short urban commutes more attractive. Plus: urban consumer amenities (non-tradeable, only in city centres) are valued disproportionately by high-income young professionals.

**Limitation:** Also need Brueckner & Rosenthal (2010) housing ageing cycle and Guerrieri et al. (2013) neighbourhood externalities to fully explain gentrification.

---

### Part (c) — Welfare: winners and losers from gentrification [30 marks]

**Treat as welfare question (not descriptive) — mock feedback explicitly says weak answers stop at "owners win, renters lose".**

**Homeowners:**
- N (neighbourhood quality) ↑ → property value ↑ → capital gain
- Can stay (enjoy amenities) or sell (realise gain)
- Only cost: loss of established social network/community
- **Unambiguously better off**
- Evidence: Guerrieri et al. (2013) — Harlem prices +**130%** in 2000–2006 vs Midtown +45%

**Renters — depends on mobility costs and whether they stay or leave:**

| Scenario | Outcome |
|----------|---------|
| No mobility costs | Can relocate to preferred neighbourhood → approximately welfare neutral |
| Mobility costs + renter leaves | Budget reduced by moving cost → welfare falls |
| Mobility costs + renter stays | Forced to consume N > N* (too much neighbourhood quality) → welfare loss unless: |
| Stays + values amenities | Positive externality offsets forced overconsumption |
| Stays + preferences adjust | Long-run preference adjustment reduces welfare loss |

**Empirical evidence:**
- **US** (Ellen & O'Regan 2011): gentrification → modest displacement; change driven by new entrants, not displacement of incumbents. Stayers often see income gains, lower crime, better schools.
- **UK** (Waights 2018): significant displacement of original residents.
- Context matters: US vs UK outcomes differ substantially.

**Political economy:**
- Homeowners = "homevoters" → support neighbourhood-raising policies (Ahlfeldt & Maennig 2015)
- Renters = "leasevoters" → oppose gentrification policies

# PART 2 — WT (Hilber)

## WT ESSAY + PARTITIONED: Real Estate Cycles
> 2012/13 Partitioned, 2018/19 Essay | Topics: WT4, WT5, WT6

### Part (a) — Two theories of real estate cycles [40 marks]

**Choose Theory 1 + Theory 2 — recommend: H&V (fundamentals) + Genesove & Mayer (behavioural)**

---

**Theory 1: Exogenous Demand Shocks + Supply Constraints — Hilber & Vermeulen (2016)**

*Mechanism:*
Real estate cycles can be fundamental when demand shocks (income, employment, population) interact with inelastic housing supply. Elastic supply → demand shocks → quantity adjustment → stable prices. Inelastic supply → demand shocks → large price swings → creates serial correlation and mean reversion.

*Two types of supply constraint (H&V 2016):*
- **Physical constraints**: undevelopable land (water bodies, steep slopes) — measured by Saiz (2010) topographic index
- **Regulatory constraints**: planning restrictions — measured by H&V using 1947–79 planning vote data

*Key findings (H&V 2016, England):*
- Regulatory constraint price elasticity: **−0.13** (1% stricter → 0.13% higher prices)
- Physical constraint elasticity: **−0.09**
- Counterfactual: if England had US-level planning flexibility → house prices **35% lower**
- Regulatory constraints dominate in South-East England

*Predictions:* Price cycles larger in constrained markets; relatively stable in unconstrained markets.

*Evidence:* San Francisco (inelastic) → extreme boom-bust; Columbus OH (elastic) → near-zero price volatility.

---

**Theory 2: Loss Aversion — Genesove & Mayer (2001, QJE)**

*Mechanism:*
Based on Kahneman-Tversky prospect theory: sellers facing nominal losses (purchase price > current market value) experience asymmetric disutility. They resist realising a loss → post higher asking prices → price stickiness in downturns → market freezes.

*Research design:* Boston condo market, early 1990s downturn. Compare sellers facing nominal losses (high LTV) vs sellers not facing losses.

*Key findings:*
- Loss-facing sellers ask **3–18% more** than comparable sellers
- Achieve **3–7% higher** transaction prices
- Stay on market significantly longer
- Effect stronger at higher LTV (deeper paper loss)

*Predictions:* Downturns marked by: (i) price stickiness (slow to clear), (ii) transaction volume collapse, (iii) prolonged adjustment periods.

---

**Other theories (know briefly in case needed):**
- **Cobweb model (Wheaton 1999):** construction lags → developers respond to past prices → build too much when market peaks → supply glut → crash. Especially relevant in commercial real estate (longer planning/construction times).
- **Irrational exuberance (Case & Shiller):** buyers extrapolate past price rises → buy expecting gains → momentum beyond fundamentals.
- **Liquidity constraints (Ortalo-Magné & Rady 2006):** credit availability amplifies cycles through first-time buyer entry.
- **Option theory (Grenadier 1995):** high uncertainty → delayed construction → supply shortfall amplifies upswing.

---

### Part (b) — Fundamentals vs deviation from equilibrium [40 marks]

**Three empirical approaches:**

**1. Capozza et al. (2004) — reduced-form test:**
Regress local price growth on fundamentals (income, population, construction costs) and lagged prices. Test whether serial correlation and mean reversion persist *above and beyond* fundamentals.
- Finding: prices above trend continue upward (momentum); eventually mean-revert (fundamentals anchor)
- Difficulty: cannot distinguish rational forward-looking expectations from irrational extrapolation

**2. H&V (2016) — structural IV approach:**
Regress house prices on demand fundamentals (instrumented with Bartik shift-share) and supply constraints. Isolate the residual (potential deviation).
- Bartik instrument: predicted local demand shock = Σ (local industry share × national industry growth)
- Exclusion restriction: national industry trends affect local housing through income only, not through construction costs
- Difficulty: requires valid instruments; exclusion restriction may be violated

**3. Case & Shiller (1988, 1989, 2003) — survey approach:**
Ask homebuyers about price expectations directly. In boom markets, buyers expect further rises → evidence of extrapolative (irrational) expectations.
- Difficulty: stated expectations may not reflect actual trading behaviour

**Conclusion:** Both fundamentals AND deviations matter. In constrained markets, demand shocks create large price movements; behavioural factors (extrapolation, loss aversion) amplify and prolong them.

---

### Part (c) — Las Vegas & Phoenix paradox [20 marks]

**The puzzle:** Both cities have elastic supply (flat terrain, minimal regulation) yet experienced extreme boom (2000–05) and crash (2005–09).

**Explanation:**

1. **Speculative/non-fundamental demand (DeFusco et al. 2013; Nathanson & Zwick 2013):** Low interest rates + loose subprime credit attracted investor buyers expecting capital gains (not housing services). This demand is *not anchored to fundamentals*. When credit dried up post-2006 → demand collapsed.

2. **Cobweb dynamics (Wheaton 1999):** Even with elastic supply, there is a construction lag. Price signal in 2004–05 → massive supply response → supply reached market after demand had already collapsed → severe price crash + inventory glut.

3. **Key insight — "elastic supply paradox":** Supply elasticity prevents *fundamental demand*-driven price cycles, but cannot prevent cycles driven by *speculative, non-fundamental demand*. LV/PHX had both price and quantity volatility (unlike SF which had price volatility but little quantity change).

---

### Essay version: "To what extent can fundamentals explain cycles?"

**Structure:**
1. **Intro:** Real estate cycles = serial correlation + mean reversion. Both fundamental and non-fundamental explanations exist.
2. **Fundamentals side:** H&V (2016): supply constraints + demand shocks → large persistent price swings. 35% counterfactual gap shows fundamentals matter enormously.
3. **Non-fundamental side:** Loss aversion (Genesove & Mayer) → price stickiness downward. Irrational exuberance (Case & Shiller) → extrapolative momentum. Liquidity constraints (Ortalo-Magné & Rady) → credit amplification. Option value (Grenadier) → delayed supply response.
4. **Conclusion:** Fundamentals are necessary but not sufficient. In constrained markets, they interact with behavioural amplifiers to create severe cycles. In unconstrained markets (Houston), fundamentals alone → rapid price stability.

---

## WT ESSAY: Land Use Regulation — Market Failure vs Policy Failure
> Mock 2025/26 Q3 | Topic: WT2

### What the question asks
Paul Cheshire (2013): "Planning serves valuable ends... but in Britain, problems of policy failure are greater than those of market failure."
Define market failure and policy failure in LUR; critically discuss.

### Essay structure

**Introduction:**
Land use regulation (LUR) may be justified by genuine market failures in land markets, but regulation is not determined by a benevolent planner — it emerges from a political process shaped by vested interests. This essay defines both failure types, evaluates the theoretical case for each, and assesses Cheshire's empirical claim.

---

**Part 1 — Market failure in LUR (4 sources)**

1. **Negative externalities:** Development creates congestion, pollution, visual intrusion → private developers over-build relative to social optimum
2. **Positive externalities / public goods:** Historic buildings, parks, green belts → non-excludable, non-rival → market under-provides
3. **Coordination failures (hold-out problem):** Land assembly requires agreement from multiple owners → strategic hold-out by any single owner can block socially beneficial redevelopment
4. **Information asymmetries:** Planners may have better information about infrastructure capacity

These failures justify *some* LUR — Cheshire acknowledges: "Planning serves valuable ends."

---

**Part 2 — Policy failure in LUR (2 mechanisms)**

**1. Homevoter hypothesis (Fischel 2000):**
- Housing = primary asset for middle-class households → they treat it as "undiversifiable human capital"
- Homeowners systematically vote/lobby against new development to protect property values
- → Planning processes capture homeowner interests, not aggregate welfare
- → Produces *excessive* regulation beyond social optimum

**2. Influential landowner hypothesis (Hilber & Robert-Nicoud 2013):**
- Already-developed landowners lobby for restrictions → supply constrained → land rents rise → existing owners benefit
- Undeveloped landowners lobby for development rights → competing interests
- Prediction: already-developed (constrained) areas → more restrictive planning → larger cycles

**Information problem:** Even a benevolent planner cannot determine optimal amount of preservation — this generates systematic over- or under-regulation.

---

**Part 3 — Empirical evidence**

*Costs of LUR (policy failure side):*
- **H&V (2016):** counterfactual of US-like planning flexibility → English house prices **35% lower**
- **Cheshire & Hilber (2008):** London West End office space **809% more expensive** than comparable US cities (regulatory tax = 809% of construction cost)
- **Hsieh & Moretti (2019):** removing US housing constraints post-1964 → aggregate GDP **2% higher** per year (eliminating spatial misallocation)
- **Cheshire et al. (2018):** 1 SD increase in planning refusal rate → vacancy rate +**23%**, commuting distance +**6.1%**
- **Hilber et al. (2019):** historic preservation → increases energy consumption per household

*Benefits of LUR (market failure side):*
- Preservation studies (Been et al. 2016; Koster et al. 2016; Ahlfeldt 2017): some positive capitalisation of spillover effects near protected buildings
- Caveat: capitalisation ≠ welfare maximisation (only marginal buyer benefit; misses aggregate supply effects)

*Net welfare effect:*
- Turner et al. (2014) and Cheshire & Sheppard (2002): UK LUR is net-negative — DWL of excessive regulation > DWL of market solution

---

**Conclusion:**
Cheshire's claim is well-supported. Genuine market failures justify *some* planning, but the political economy of homeowners and influential landowners produces *excessive* restriction in Britain. Empirical evidence (35% price premium; 2% GDP loss) suggests policy failure dominates. Implication: not abolish planning but redesign it to work *with* markets — e.g., replace restrictive zoning with Pigouvian taxes, streamline consent processes.

---

## WT PARTITIONED: Housing Supply Elasticity — Identification & Evidence
> Mock 2025/26 Q4, 2013/14 Q6 | Topic: WT3

### Part (a) — The identification problem [30 marks]

**Core problem:**
We only observe equilibrium (P*, Q*) which lies simultaneously on *both* the supply curve and the demand curve. A simple OLS regression of Q on P estimates neither — it is a weighted average of both (simultaneity bias).

**Why OLS fails:** P is jointly determined with Q in equilibrium → Cov(P, ε) ≠ 0 → OLS estimate of β is biased.

**Intuition:** When we observe P and Q moving together, we cannot tell whether demand shifted (tracing out supply curve) or supply shifted (tracing out demand curve) or both shifted simultaneously.

**Additional difficulties:**
- Housing stock is non-stationary (trending) → regressing levels on levels → spurious correlation → must use changes (ΔP, ΔQ)
- Stock elasticity vs flow elasticity are very different: Mayer & Somerville (2000) find stock elasticity = **0.08** but new starts elasticity = **6.3**
- Data limitations, market boundary definition, functional form choices

---

### Part (b) — IV / 2SLS methodology [40 marks]

**Solution: Instrumental Variables (IV) / Two-Stage Least Squares (2SLS)**

To estimate the *supply* curve, need an instrument Z that:
1. **Shifts demand** (relevant: correlated with P) — keeping supply fixed, traces out supply slope
2. **Only affects supply through price** (exclusion restriction: Z ⊥ ε_supply)

**The Bartik shift-share instrument:**
Z_jt = Σ_k (share of industry k in region j at base year) × (national employment growth in industry k_t)

- Captures exogenous local demand variation from national industry trends
- Exclusion restriction: national trends affect local housing demand through income; do not directly affect construction costs or planning constraints

**Two-stage procedure:**
- First stage: ΔP_t = β₀ + β₁·ΔZ_t + controls + μ_t → get fitted Δ P̂_t
- Second stage: ΔQ_t = α₀ + α₁·Δ P̂_t + supply shifters + ε_t → α₁ = supply elasticity

**Key estimates:**

| Study | Elasticity | Notes |
|-------|-----------|-------|
| Mayer & Somerville (2000) | Stock: **0.08**; Starts: **6.3** | Stock inelastic — housing is durable |
| Saiz (2010) | Average: **1.7** (range 0.6–5.5) | US MSAs; topographic IV |
| Hilber & Mense (2025) | Average: **1.16** (range 0.15–3.6) | UK; significantly lower than US |
| Hilber & Mayer (2009) | Urban: **0.014**; Rural: **0.16** | Land availability heterogeneity |

---

### Part (c) — Estimating equation [20 marks]

**Second stage (supply elasticity equation):**
> ΔQ^S_t = α₀ + α₁·Δ P̂_t + α₂·(ΔConstruction Cost_t) + α₃·(Planning Constraint_t) + ε_t

- ΔQ^S = change in housing stock or new starts
- Δ P̂ = predicted price change from first stage (Bartik-instrumented)
- α₁ = supply price elasticity
- Why changes: removes non-stationarity (unit roots) in levels

**First stage:**
> ΔP_t = β₀ + β₁·ΔZ_t (Bartik) + β₂·(Supply Shifters_t) + μ_t

---

### Part (d) — Locational determinants of supply elasticity [30 marks]

**Two categories (H&V 2016):**

**1. Physical / topographic constraints:**
- Water bodies (coastline, lakes), steep slopes (>15% gradient) → limit buildable land
- Saiz (2010): constructs "Residential Land Available" index from GIS
- More undevelopable land → scarcer buildable land → higher land prices → lower supply response

**2. Regulatory constraints:**
- Zoning, height limits, conservation areas, green belts
- H&V (2016): measured using 1947–79 local planning vote data
- **H&V finding: regulatory constraints > physical constraints** in explaining English house price cycles
- Endogeneity concern: H&V instrument using long-lagged (pre-determined) regulatory measures

**Hilber & Mayer (2009) — within-city heterogeneity:**
- Urban areas (low undeveloped land): elasticity = **0.014** ≈ zero
- Rural areas (high undeveloped land): elasticity = **0.16**

**Three channels why less land → lower elasticity:**
1. *Cost channel:* Building on constrained/difficult terrain is more expensive
2. *Political economy:* Dense areas have more homeowners with NIMBY incentives → stricter planning
3. *Real options:* Scarce land has higher option value (Grenadier 1995) → developers wait → delayed supply response

**Policy value of knowing elasticity:**
- Elastic market (Houston): demand shock → quantity ↑, price stable → predictable rental yields, low capital gain risk
- Inelastic market (London): demand shock → price ↑↑ → appreciation potential but downside risk
- Hilber & Turner (2014): housing subsidies (Help-to-Buy, mortgage interest deduction) have **zero average** effect on homeownership nationally; **negative effect** in inelastic markets (subsidy → demand ↑ → price ↑↑ → deposit requirement ↑ → ownership ↓)

---

## WT ESSAY: Tiebout Model & Fiscal Capitalization
> GY457 Exam 2012 Q4 | Topic: WT1

### What the question asks
"Consumer mobility and inter-jurisdictional competition lead to an efficient provision of local public services. Critically discuss with reference to the literature following Tiebout (1956)."

### Essay structure

**Introduction:**
Tiebout (1956) proposed that decentralised provision of local public goods achieves allocative efficiency through residential mobility: households "vote with their feet" by selecting the jurisdiction matching their preferred tax-service bundle. This generates inter-jurisdictional competition analogous to private market competition. While theoretically elegant, the proposition requires restrictive assumptions rarely met in practice. Post-Tiebout empirical work on fiscal capitalization offers partial support, but reveals important limitations and regressive distributional consequences.

---

**Part 1 — The Tiebout mechanism**

Three forces driving efficiency:
1. **Variety:** multiple jurisdictions offer differentiated tax-service packages
2. **Sorting:** households reveal preferences through location choices (not voting)
3. **Competition:** jurisdictions compete for residents → disciplined, efficient provision

**5 key assumptions required:**

| Assumption | Why it matters |
|----------|---------------|
| Perfect information | Households know all jurisdictions' offerings |
| Costless mobility | No moving costs; free sorting |
| Many jurisdictions | Sufficient variety for preference matching |
| No spillovers | Public goods purely local; no cross-boundary externalities |
| No scale economies | Optimal jurisdiction size well-defined |

---

**Part 2 — Fiscal capitalization as empirical test**

**The testable prediction:** Fiscal variables (taxes, service quality) should be fully capitalised into property prices — households bid up prices in high-service jurisdictions and bid down in high-tax ones.

**Oates (1969):** First systematic test, New Jersey municipalities:
- Property tax ↑ £1,000 → house price **−£664** (≈67% capitalisation)
- Higher school spending → significantly higher house prices
→ Broadly consistent with Tiebout sorting prediction

**School quality capitalisation:** Literature finds **1.3%–10%** house price premium per 1 standard deviation improvement in school test scores (Gibbons et al. 2013; Cheshire & Sheppard 2004; Black 1999 — sharp price discontinuities at school district boundaries).

---

**Part 3 — Supply elasticity moderates capitalisation (critical innovation)**

**Hilber & Mayer (2009):**
- Urban areas (inelastic supply, low undeveloped land): school spending capitalisation = **33%**
- Rural areas (elastic supply, abundant land): capitalisation = only **10%**

**Mechanism:** In supply-constrained markets, improved school quality → demand ↑ → *price ↑↑* (not quantity ↑). Fiscal benefits accrue almost entirely to existing **homeowners and landlords**, not renters.

```
Supply-inelastic (London): better school → price ↑↑, quantity unchanged
  → Homeowners capture full surplus; renters pay higher rent

Supply-elastic (Houston): better school → quantity ↑, price modest ↑
  → Fiscal benefits spread to new residents through housing supply
```

**Implication:** Even where Tiebout sorting occurs, benefits are not efficiently distributed — they are captured by asset owners.

---

**Part 4 — Critical assessment**

**Where Tiebout partially works:**
- Sorting does occur: income/preferences correlated with jurisdiction choice
- Black (1999): sharp house price discontinuities at school district boundaries → confirms households value school quality

**Where Tiebout fails — assumption violations:**

**(a) Mobility costs are high:** Moving involves financial costs (stamp duty, transaction costs), social capital losses, job disruption. Low-income households face higher *relative* mobility costs → cannot sort freely → efficiency breaks down.

**(b) Externalities exist:** Education generates cross-jurisdiction positive spillovers (better-educated workforce benefits neighbouring areas) → Tiebout ignores this → under-provision of education.

**(c) Scale economies:** Specialised hospitals, transit networks require large catchment areas → small jurisdictions cannot provide efficiently.

**(d) Race to the bottom (Wilson 1986):** Jurisdictional competition on *business* taxes may deplete public finances rather than improve them.

**Distributional critique (high-mark point):**
- Tiebout sorting stratifies communities by income → richer jurisdictions provide better schools → **intergenerational inequality reinforced**
- Government subsidies (central grants to deprived areas) capitalised into land values → benefit landowners, not target households (Hilber et al. 2011)
- Policy implication: **"Help people, not places"** — place-based subsidies are inefficient in supply-constrained markets; direct transfers are more effective

---

**Conclusion:**
The Tiebout proposition contains genuine insight: mobility and competition create incentives for efficient local public good provision, and fiscal capitalization evidence confirms households value the net fiscal surplus. However, the full efficiency result requires assumptions routinely violated in practice — especially costless mobility and no spillovers. Supply constraints further concentrate benefits on asset owners. The model is best understood as a useful benchmark: it illuminates the forces at work, while departures from its assumptions explain much of the observed inequality in local public service provision.
