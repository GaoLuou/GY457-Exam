**Mock Exam 2025/26 — Essay (100 marks):**
> "Traditional urban economics models view cities as aligned around a single centre, the so-called Central Business District (CBD). In this class of models, firms concentrate in the CBD, despite paying higher rent. Critically assess this statement, with reference to economic theory and empirical evidence. Should we expect cities to become more polycentric? Discuss."

## Merit-Level Essay Structure

### Introduction (3–4 sentences)
The monocentric city model (Alonso 1964; Mills 1967; Muth 1969) provides the foundational framework for urban spatial structure: land goes to the highest bidder, commercial users cluster near the centre, and rent declines with distance. While this captures important mechanisms, it imposes a single exogenous agglomeration point and cannot explain the emergence of secondary employment centres observed in modern cities. This essay critically assesses the theoretical basis for CBD concentration, examines decentralisation pressures, and discusses conditions under which cities become polycentric.

### Part 1: Why Firms Cluster in the CBD

**Bid-rent logic:**
In the AMM model, a firm's bid-rent is the maximum rent it is willing to pay at each location while earning zero profit. Productivity spillovers decline with distance from the CBD (Ahlfeldt & Barr 2022): A_i = a·e^(−θD_i), where θ is the spatial decay parameter. A higher θ means spillovers are more localised, generating a steeper firm bid-rent gradient. Since commercial bid-rent exceeds residential bid-rent near the centre, land goes to commercial users there, producing the monocentric structure.

**Why the CBD specifically matters:**
CBD concentration is not merely about transport access but about **knowledge spillovers** (extremely localised — street or building level; Rosenthal & Strange 2020) and **labour market pooling** (effective at the commuting zone level). Ahlfeldt et al. (2020) find that CBD dominance is driven by knowledge-intensive tradeable services (finance, law, professional services) with high θ; manufacturing has low θ and disperses.

**Empirical support:** Liotta et al. (2022): rent declines at −1.4%/km globally. Heblich et al. (2020): removing London's rail network reduces land values by 50%, confirming transport-access value of centrality.

### Part 2: Limitations — Endogenous Agglomeration

The monocentric model assumes CBD agglomeration is exogenous. Ahlfeldt & Wendland (2013) show that firms generate **bilateral spillovers** between each other: A_i = a_i·e^(−θD_i)·e^(βZ_i), where Z_i captures proximity-weighted output of all other firms. This creates **self-reinforcing clusters** anywhere, not just the historical CBD.

When bilateral spillovers are strong, an initial shock (e.g., some firms relocating to a suburb) can trigger a new stable cluster — producing **multiple equilibria**. The outcome depends on history and initial conditions (analogous to Krugman 1991 core-periphery). This generates **path dependence**: NYC's dense CBD reflects its 1900 population of 3.4 million and early transit investment; LA's dispersed structure reflects its late urbanisation (100k in 1900) without a comparable rail network (Ahlfeldt et al. 2020).

### Part 3: Decentralisation and Sub-Centres

**Employment decentralisation evidence:** Glaeser & Kahn (2001): fewer than 29% of jobs are within 3 miles of the CBD in most US metros. Baum-Snow et al. (2017) — China: ring roads displaced 50% of industrial employment.

**Sub-centres (McMillen & Smith 2003):** Sub-centres emerge organically (multiple decision-makers) when population ↑ and congestion ↑, as predicted by Fujita & Ogawa (1982). Identification: employment density peaks above the distance-predicted level. Cities with older housing stock and higher population have more sub-centres.

**Edge cities (Henderson & Mitra 1996):** Built by a single developer who internalises agglomeration gains. Developer chooses location K (capacity and distance from CBD): closer = more CBD spillovers but higher wages/land costs. Tyson's Corner (VA): 28m sq ft Class A office space vs DC's 17m. Distinguishing feature: net commuting destination (more jobs than bedrooms).

### Part 4: Will Cities Become More Polycentric?

Yes, with qualifications:
1. Cities where manufacturing dominates (low θ) → already dispersed.
2. Knowledge-intensive cities (high θ) → CBD advantage remains strong.
3. **Transport improvements** (t↓) flatten bid-rent gradients → suburbanisation and potential for sub-centres.
4. **Rising congestion** (central cities) → decentralisation pressure.
5. **Remote work** (emerging post-pandemic) → potentially weaken CBD-specific advantages.

Empirical evidence suggests decentralisation is ongoing but uneven — not all cities converge to the same structure.

### Conclusion
The monocentric model correctly captures the bid-rent mechanism and productivity basis for CBD concentration. However, it fails to account for endogenous agglomeration forces and bilateral spillovers, which can sustain multiple stable clusters. Evidence confirms partial polycentricity in most cities, with sub-centres forming organically and edge cities built strategically. Whether polycentricity deepens depends on the decay rate of spillovers (θ), congestion costs, and historical infrastructure lock-in. Knowledge cities will remain relatively monocentric; transport-accessible, lower-spillover cities will continue to decentralise.

## 中文答题思路

**逻辑链**：AMM竞租逻辑（bid-rent斜率差异 → CBD商业集中）→ 为什么具体是CBD（θ参数：知识溢出极局部）→ AMM局限（外生CBD假设 → 内生双向溢出 → 多重均衡）→ 去中心化证据（29%就业，中国环形道路）→ 次级中心 vs 边缘城市（有机 vs 战略）→ 会更多元中心，但程度取决于θ+历史条件

**Mock考试最常见错误（要避免）**：
- 只说"企业集聚CBD因为贵/方便"——要讲θ衰减和bilateral spillovers
- 不讲为什么CBD"具体"重要（知识溢出极局部）
- 结论说"会更多元中心"不联系理论，要说什么条件下会/不会
