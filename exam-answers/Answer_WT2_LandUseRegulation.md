# WT Part 2: Land Use Regulation — Market Failure vs Policy Failure

## Past Exam Question

**Mock Exam 2025/26 — Essay (100 marks):**
> Paul Cheshire (2013) argued: "Planning serves valuable ends. Land market failure is potentially a serious problem... The evidence very strongly indicates, however, that in Britain at least, problems of policy failure are greater than those of market failure."
>
> Explain the meanings of "market failure" and "policy failure" in the context of land use regulation, and critically discuss this statement with reference to the relevant theoretical and empirical literature.

## Merit-Level Essay Structure

### Introduction
Land use regulation (LUR) occupies a central place in urban economics because land markets are prone to multiple forms of market failure that may justify government intervention. However, regulation is not determined by a benevolent social planner — it emerges from a political process subject to vested interests and informational constraints. This essay defines market and policy failure in the LUR context, evaluates the theoretical case for each, and assesses Cheshire's claim using empirical evidence.

### Part 1: Defining Market Failure in LUR

**Market failure** occurs when unregulated land markets fail to deliver economically efficient outcomes. In LUR, four sources are relevant:

1. **Negative externalities:** Development creates congestion, environmental degradation, and visual intrusion (e.g., tall buildings blocking views). Without regulation, developers over-build relative to the social optimum.

2. **Positive externalities / public goods:** Historic buildings generate amenity value for all (non-excludable, non-rival) — markets under-provide preservation. Similarly, parks and green belts provide public goods that markets will not supply at optimal levels.

3. **Coordination failures:** Land assembly requires agreement from multiple landowners. Strategic hold-out problems mean even mutually beneficial redevelopment can fail without a coordinating mechanism (e.g., infrastructure provision or block redevelopment).

4. **Information asymmetries:** Planners may have better information about infrastructure capacity than developers.

These failures justify *some* form of LUR — Cheshire acknowledges this: "Planning serves valuable ends."

### Part 2: Defining Policy Failure in LUR

**Policy failure** occurs when government intervention introduces inefficiencies that exceed the market failure it was meant to correct. Two theoretical mechanisms:

1. **Homevoter hypothesis (Fischel 2000):** Homeowners constitute the majority of local planning committees and voters. Since housing is their primary asset, they systematically oppose new development that might depress local property values. This produces *excessive* regulation — beyond the social optimum — because planners internalise homeowners' interests, not aggregate welfare.

2. **Influential landowner hypothesis (Hilber & Robert-Nicoud 2013):** Large undeveloped landowners lobby for restrictions that protect existing asset values. Zoning restricts supply → land rents rise → existing owners benefit. Regulatory stringency is endogenous to the political economy.

**Information problem:** Even a benevolent planner cannot easily determine the optimal amount of preservation — how much green belt is socially optimal? This information gap leads to either over- or under-regulation.

### Part 3: Empirical Assessment — Is Policy Failure > Market Failure in Britain?

**Evidence on costs of LUR (policy failure side):**

- **House prices:** Hilber & Vermeulen (2016) — regulatory constraints raise English house prices; counterfactual: if England had US planning flexibility, prices ~35% lower. Cheshire & Hilber (2008) — office space 800% more expensive in London West End than comparable US city.
- **Output and productivity:** Hsieh & Moretti (2019) — if US cities had removed housing constraints post-1964, GDP would be 2% higher per year. Duranton & Puga (2023) similar evidence.
- **Vacancies and commuting:** Cheshire et al. (2018) — LUR increases vacancy rates and commuting distances.
- **Energy consumption:** Hilber et al. (2019) — historic preservation increases energy use (old buildings are inefficient), raising social carbon costs.

**Evidence on benefits of LUR (market failure correction):**
- Preservation studies (Been et al. 2016; Koster et al. 2016): Some evidence of positive spillover capitalisation into house prices near protected buildings. But: capitalisation ≠ welfare maximisation (only captures benefit to marginal buyer; misses aggregate supply effects and non-market values).

**Net welfare:**
- Turner et al. (2014) and Cheshire & Sheppard (2002) both suggest UK LUR is too restrictive — DWL of excessive regulation > DWL of market solution.
- The social optimum (MSC = MSB) appears to lie to the *left* of the politically determined outcome.

### Part 4: Critical Assessment of Cheshire's Claim

Cheshire's claim — that policy failure > market failure in Britain — is well-supported empirically, but with important caveats:

**Supporting the claim:**
- The scale of supply restriction in the UK (H&V 2016 counterfactual: 35% price reduction) implies enormous social costs.
- Political economy theories (homevoter, influential landowner) explain why democratic processes produce excessive restriction.
- H&V (2016) find regulatory constraints dominate physical constraints in England — regulation is endogenous to political economy, not just geography.

**Caveats:**
- Market failure is genuinely significant: without coordination mechanisms, positive amenity externalities (historic buildings, green space) would be under-provided.
- Evidence from hedonic studies shows some capitalisation of preservation benefits — suggesting real welfare gains from some regulation.
- The claim is context-specific: applies most strongly to England and similar restrictive systems. Less clearly true in the US (where physical constraints may dominate in high-price cities).

### Conclusion
Economic theory identifies genuine market failures in land markets that justify planning intervention. However, the political economy of planning — driven by homevoter interests and influential landowners — produces systematic over-regulation in Britain. The empirical evidence strongly supports Cheshire's claim: the costs of policy failure (reduced housing supply, lower productivity, higher prices) appear to exceed the benefits of market failure correction. The implication is not to abolish planning but to redesign it to "work with markets" — e.g., replacing restrictive zoning with Pigouvian taxes, streamlining consent processes, and making benefits more transparent.

## 中文答题思路

**核心框架**：这道题要求比较**市场失灵**（不干预 → 太少保护/太多开发）vs **政策失灵**（干预过度 → 太少建设）。Cheshire说英国的政策失灵>市场失灵。

**答题逻辑链**：
1. 定义市场失灵：外部性（负：拥堵；正：历史建筑）+ 公共品 + 协调失灵
2. 定义政策失灵：homevoter hypothesis（房主多投票 → 反对开发保护房价）+ influential landowner（地主游说限制供给）+ 信息问题
3. 实证：成本（H&V: 35%更低房价；Hsieh & Moretti: GDP +2%）vs 收益（保护资本化，但只是局部证据）
4. 结论：支持Cheshire——政策失灵超过市场失灵，但保留场景特殊性

**Mock考试主要错误**：
- 没有讲政治经济学理论（homevoter + influential landowner）——这是"一阶概念缺口"
- 没有真正讨论"政策失灵 > 市场失灵"的claim，只是分别描述两者
- 证据引用停留在anecdotal层面，没有H&V等具体数字
