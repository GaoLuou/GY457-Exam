## Part (a): Two Explanations for Suburbanisation — AMM Framework (30 marks)

The AMM monocentric city model predicts that **rising income** and **falling commuting costs** both cause suburbanisation by flattening the bid-rent gradient.

**Mechanism 1 — Rising income (y↑):**
The bid-rent gradient is ∂p_q/∂x = −t/q. Housing is a normal good, so y↑ → q(y)↑. As q rises, the gradient −t/q **flattens** (becomes less negative). The bid-rent curve rotates around a pivot point x̂, falling at the centre and rising at the suburbs. The urban boundary x̄ expands outward — the city suburbanises. In the closed-city case, central density falls and suburban density rises. Evidence: Baum-Snow (2007) finds each additional highway through a US city centre reduces central population by 18%.

**Mechanism 2 — Falling commuting costs (t↓):**
Directly reduces the gradient slope −t/q: as t falls, the gradient flattens by the same mechanism. Households move to the suburbs where housing is cheaper, enjoying the same utility at lower commuting cost (equivalent to an income increase). Transport infrastructure is thus a key driver of suburbanisation. Evidence: Heblich et al. (2020) show that London steam railways triggered the first mass suburbanisation; removing the network would reduce population and land values by 50%.

**Income segregation note:** Under monetary commuting costs, rich households have flatter bid-rent (higher q → less sensitive to distance) → live in suburbs. Poor households have steeper bid-rent → live in city centre. This explains the 20th-century pattern of wealthy suburbanisation.

## Part (b): One Theory Explaining Both Suburbanisation AND Reversal — LeRoy & Sonstiele (1983) (40 marks)

**The 4-Stage Transportation Cost Cycle:**

LeRoy & Sonstiele (1983) argue that the residential location of the rich follows a predictable cycle tied to the adoption of new transport modes. New transport technologies are initially fast but expensive, adopted first by the rich.

| Stage | Transport | Rich | Poor |
|-------|-----------|------|------|
| 1. Paradise | Slow, cheap only | Centre | Periphery |
| 2. Paradise Lost | Fast mode appears (expensive) | **Suburbs** | Centre |
| 3. Re-gentrification | Fast mode becomes affordable | **Centre** (return) | Suburbs |
| 4. Paradise Regained | All use fast mode | Centre | Suburbs |

**Key assumption:** Income elasticity of housing demand < income elasticity of marginal commuting cost (rich value time more than big houses).

**Application to the US:**
- 1900s: Automobiles expensive → only rich suburbanise → Stage 2 (white suburbanisation / "white flight").
- Late 20th century: Cars near-universal (92% of US households own a car by 2017) → poor also suburb-accessible → Stage 3/4.
- Rich return to city centres (gentrification): higher time value + city amenities (restaurants, culture) → Stage 3.

**Mechanism:** As fast transport becomes universal, the rich no longer gain a differential advantage from using it. Their higher time value makes short commutes (living centrally) relatively more attractive. Simultaneously, urban amenities (non-tradeable consumer goods — Couture & Handbury 2020) cluster in city centres and are valued more by high-income young professionals.

**Limitation:** The model abstracts from housing durability (Brueckner & Rosenthal 2010) and neighbourhood externalities (Guerrieri et al. 2013), which also contribute to the gentrification cycle.

## Part (c): Welfare Analysis — Winners and Losers from Gentrification (30 marks)

**Mock feedback says:** Treat as a welfare question, not descriptive. Include movers vs stayers, short-run vs long-run, amenity valuation, empirical nuance.

**Homeowners:**
Rising neighbourhood quality N↑ → property values ↑ → capital gain. Homeowners can: (i) stay and enjoy the neighbourhood improvement, or (ii) sell and realise the gain. The only cost is potential loss of social capital (established community networks). In welfare terms, homeowners are **unambiguously better off** in expectation. Evidence: Guerrieri et al. (2013) find Harlem house prices rose 130% in 2000–2006 (vs 45% in Midtown), benefiting existing owners.

**Renters:**
Analysis depends on mobility costs:
- *No mobility costs:* Renters can relocate to optimal neighbourhood; welfare approximately neutral.
- *With mobility costs, renter leaves:* Budget reduced by moving cost (Y↓) → welfare falls.
- *With mobility costs, renter stays:* Forced to consume N_gentrification > N*, the preferred quantity. Welfare loss, unless:
  - Renter values the improved amenities (positive externality effect)
  - Preferences adjust over time ("preference adjustment")

In practice: **US evidence** (Ellen & O'Regan 2011) — gentrification has modest displacement effects; neighbourhood change driven more by new entrants than by displacement of incumbents. Stayers often experience income gains, better schools, lower crime. **UK evidence** (Waights 2018) — significant displacement of original residents. Context matters.

**Net welfare:** Renters who stay and value amenities may gain; renters who are forced to move lose. The distributional impact is highly unequal and context-dependent.

**Policy implication:** Homeowners are "homevoters" who support gentrification; renters are "leasevoters" who oppose neighbourhood-raising policies (Ahlfeldt & Maennig 2015). This political economy tension shapes local planning outcomes.

## 中文答题思路

**(a)** 不要只说"收入高了就搬郊区"——要通过AMM机制解释：y↑→q↑→梯度变平→城市边界扩张。通勤成本↓同理。用Baum-Snow(-18%)做证据。

**(b)** 选LeRoy-Sonstiele作为"唯一能解释两者的理论"：郊区化=阶段2（快速交通出现但富人独享），绅士化=阶段3（快速交通普及→富人重返城市）。一定要讲assumption（时间价值弹性 > 住房需求弹性）。

**(c)** 不要停留在"房主赢，租户输"——要讲：留守 vs 搬走，流动性成本的作用，美国vs英国差异，邻里改善对留守者的正溢出。Mock feedback明确说弱答案在这里停步太早。
